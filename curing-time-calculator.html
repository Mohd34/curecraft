The Physics of Osmotic Diffusion in Meat

When you apply salt and Prague Powder to the surface of a slab of pork belly or beef round, the cure dissolves in surface moisture and creates a steep concentration gradient.

According to Fick's Second Law of Diffusion, the rate at which solute molecules migrate through muscle tissue is governed by the diffusion coefficient of sodium chloride and sodium nitrite in meat:

Diffusion Velocity ≈ 6.35 mm (0.25 inches) per 24 hours at 38°F (3.3°C)
Effective Penetration Depth for Flat Slabs = Thickness / 2 (curing from both sides)
Minimum Safe Days = (Effective Depth in mm / 6.35) + 2 Safety Buffer Days

Why More Time Does No Harm in Equilibrium Curing

In traditional high-salt curing, leaving meat in the brine or salt rub for 3 extra days would turn it into a salt lick. But with equilibrium curing, the total amount of salt in the bag is strictly capped at your target (e.g., 2.50%). Once the core reaches 2.50% salt, net diffusion ceases.

Therefore, when using the equilibrium method, leaving your bacon in the refrigerator for 10 or 12 days instead of the calculated 7 days is actually beneficial: it ensures the salt gradient is completely uniform from surface to marrow.
