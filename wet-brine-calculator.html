Why Traditional "Cover Brines" Fail Home Cooks

Most old-fashioned recipes for corned beef or wet-cured ham instruct you to make a strong brine—often 10% to 15% salinity (using 1 to 1.5 cups of salt per gallon)—and soak the meat for "3 to 5 days".

This method relies on gradient diffusion. The exterior of the meat absorbs salt at an alarming rate, while the center lags behind. If you remove the meat 12 hours too late, the outer 1 inch is inedibly salty, requiring hours of freshwater boiling to leach out excess sodium. If you remove it 12 hours too early, the deep interior has not reached the 100+ PPM nitrite threshold required to prevent bacterial growth.

The Equilibrium Immersion Solution

In an equilibrium wet brine, you calculate salt and curing agents based on the combined mass:

Total System Mass (M_total) = Meat Weight (g) + Water Weight (g)
Prague Powder #1 (g) = M_total × 0.0025 (0.25%)
Total Target Salt (g) = M_total × Target Salinity %
Pure Salt to Add (g) = Total Target Salt - (Prague Powder × 0.9375)

Because the total amount of salt in the container matches the exact target salinity of the final meat, it is physically impossible for the meat to absorb too much salt. Once the gradient flattens out, the system reaches permanent equilibrium.
